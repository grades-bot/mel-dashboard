What is MEL and why does it matter?

The Three Pillars of MEL

MEL is a structured approach that helps organisations track progress, assess effectiveness, and apply lessons learned to improve programme design and delivery. In the development sector, MEL is not a compliance exercise — it is the foundation of strategic decision-making and accountability to beneficiaries, donors, and partners.

📊

Monitoring

Continuous, systematic collection of data against indicators and targets. Tracks what is happening throughout implementation.

  • Routine data collection
  • Progress against logframe
  • Early warning signals
🔍

Evaluation

Periodic, in-depth assessment of relevance, effectiveness, and impact. Answers whether the programme worked and why.

  • Formative & summative reviews
  • OECD DAC criteria
  • Attribution analysis
💡

Learning

Translating evidence into actionable insights. Ensures knowledge is captured, shared, and applied to improve practice.

Pillar 1

Monitoring

The continuous, systematic process of collecting and reviewing data against pre-defined indicators and targets.

Indicators & Targets

SMART indicators (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound) with baseline values and milestone targets defined in the results framework. Disaggregate by sex, age, and other relevant categories.

Data Collection Methods

Surveys, Key Informant Interviews (KIIs), Focus Group Discussions (FGDs), administrative records, remote sensing, and participatory tools. Match methods to the indicator and context.

Results Framework

A visual map linking programme inputs and activities through to outputs, outcomes, and ultimate impact — with indicators, baselines, and targets at each level.

Progress Tracking

Regular reporting cycles with dashboards and management information systems. Monitoring data should feed directly into programme decision-making, not just donor reports.

Key question Monitoring answers: "Are we doing what we said we would, and are we reaching the right people?"
Pillar 2

Evaluation

Periodic, in-depth assessments that determine whether — and why — a programme achieved its intended results.

Formative

During Implementation

Conducted while the programme is running to improve design and delivery in real time. Focuses on process quality and early results to enable course correction before it is too late.

Summative

End of Programme

Conducted at or after programme completion to assess overall results and accountability. Answers whether the intended impact was achieved and at what cost.

Pillar 3

Learning

Converting monitoring data and evaluation findings into usable knowledge that actually improves decisions and programmes.

📚

Knowledge Management

Systematic capture, storage, and dissemination of lessons learned — making organisational knowledge accessible and reusable across teams, projects, and programme cycles.

🔄

Learning Loops

Structured feedback cycles connecting evidence back to programme strategy. Single-loop learning fixes operational problems; double-loop learning questions the underlying assumptions and theory of change itself.

🧭

Learning Agenda

A prioritised set of strategic questions that guide what evidence to gather — keeping inquiry focused on decisions that matter most to the organisation, not just what is easy to measure.

Without dedicated learning time and budget, monitoring and evaluation data often sits in reports that no one reads. Learning must be planned, resourced, and championed by leadership.

How MEL Uses the Theory of Change

Each arrow in the chain represents a causal assumption. MEL tests whether those assumptions hold in practice, enabling teams to strengthen or revise the theory based on real evidence — not hope.

Define the causal pathway

Map inputs → activities → outputs → outcomes → impact with explicit assumptions at each link.

Select indicators per level

Choose indicators that test the most critical assumptions — focus on what changes, not what is easy to count.

Test and revise

Use monitoring and evaluation evidence to confirm, challenge, or update the theory throughout implementation.

MEL Plan Components

A MEL system is only as strong as the plan that underpins it. Four foundations every MEL plan must address.

Logframe / Results Framework

Indicators, baselines, targets, and means of verification for each level of the results chain. The logframe is the backbone of both monitoring and evaluation.

Data Systems & Tools

Data collection instruments, management information systems, quality assurance protocols, and reporting templates. Data systems should be appropriate to team capacity and context.

Roles & Responsibilities

Clear assignment of MEL tasks to staff, partners, and external evaluators — with timelines, authority, and accountability. Underfunded MEL teams cannot deliver.

MEL Budget

Industry standard: allocate 5–10% of total programme budget to ensure robust MEL delivery. This includes data collection, analysis, evaluation commissions, learning events, and reporting.

Adaptive Management

Closing the loop between evidence and action — ensuring what MEL surfaces is used to improve programmes, not just filed in reports.

Step 01

Collect Evidence

Gather monitoring data, evaluation findings, and learning from reflection sessions and stakeholder feedback.

Step 02

Analyse & Sense-Make

Team reflection and structured analysis to identify patterns, unexpected results, and what the evidence means for strategy.

Step 03

Decide & Adapt

Make deliberate adjustments to strategy, resources, activities, or the theory of change based on the evidence reviewed.

Step 04

Implement Changes

Execute updated approaches, communicate changes to the team and partners, and begin the monitoring cycle again.

↻ The cycle repeats continuously throughout programme implementation
Organisations that embed adaptive management see faster course correction and stronger programme outcomes over time. The key is creating institutional permission and space to act on evidence — not just collect it.

Common MEL Pitfalls

Even well-resourced organisations fall into these traps. Awareness is the first step to avoiding them.

📋

Measuring Outputs Only

Counting activities delivered without tracking whether outcomes or behaviour change actually occurred — the most widespread MEL failure in the sector.

📉

No Baseline Data

Without a baseline, it is impossible to measure change attributable to the programme. Baselines must be established before implementation begins.

📂

Data Collected but Not Used

Evidence gathered for compliance only — never reviewed in programme decisions, team reflections, or strategy meetings. Data without use is waste.

💸

Learning Underfunded

MEL budgets cover data collection but not sense-making, knowledge sharing, or learning events — leaving insight trapped in spreadsheets and inaccessible reports.

Bonus pitfall: Leaving communities and local stakeholders out of MEL design. Participatory MEL improves data quality, builds trust, and ensures findings are contextually grounded.

How to Avoid Them

Set a baseline before Day 1

Build baseline data collection into programme design and budget — not as an afterthought.

Track outcome indicators, not just outputs

Ask "So what?" after every output. What change in knowledge, behaviour, or conditions was produced?

Schedule regular reflection sessions

Monthly or quarterly learning reviews where teams interrogate the data together and agree adaptations.
